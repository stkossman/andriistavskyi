title: Writing a World Before Writing a Book
date: 2026-07-10
summary: How one rule about life, death, and consequence became the foundation of a fantasy world.
---

I started thinking about a fantasy book before I knew its full plot.

There was no complete map, carefully ordered chapter list, or final scene waiting to be written. I began with one rule:

> Suffering does not disappear. It only changes form.

In this world, the land of the living and a supernatural world are connected by a balance. When someone escapes a death that should have happened, that death does not simply vanish. It moves elsewhere and gives shape to something darker. Destroying that darkness can return the cost to the living.

The rule creates a problem without an easy heroic answer.

Saving a person may have consequences. Defeating a monster may harm someone innocent. Preserving balance may mean accepting a system built on fear and sacrifice.

Once that rule existed, the rest of the world began to grow around it.

I started asking questions:

- Who controls the boundary between the two worlds?
- Who benefits from calling the current system “balance”?
- What happens when an institution protects its authority instead of its responsibility?
- Can mercy still be right when the world punishes it?
- What kind of person would keep helping others after learning the cost?

Those questions slowly produced clans, rituals, weapons, beliefs, and characters. Their abilities could not exist only because they looked impressive. They needed to express something about the world or the person using them.

A sword could represent responsibility rather than power. A blindfold could be a form of self-control instead of a visual accessory. A character’s greatest strength could also be the reason other people fear them.

I enjoy this part of writing because it feels both creative and structural.

A fantasy world needs imagination, but it also needs consistency. Once a rule is established, I have to respect its consequences. If I change it whenever the story becomes inconvenient, the world stops feeling real. If I follow it too mechanically, the story can become predictable.

The interesting space is between those extremes.

Writing also gives me a different kind of problem from software development. Code eventually has to produce a defined result. A story can remain uncertain for much longer. A character may change the direction of a scene. An idea that seemed central may become unnecessary. A better question can matter more than an immediate answer.

I am still developing the story, and many details will probably change. I do not need to know every location, battle, or ending yet.

What I need is a foundation strong enough to keep asking the same central question:

**If a balance depends on innocent people paying the price, is preserving it really the right thing to do?**

For now, building the world around that question is the part I enjoy most.
