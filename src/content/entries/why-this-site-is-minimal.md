---
title: Why This Site Is Minimal
date: 2026-07-11
summary: A small portfolio should make it easy to find the work, not compete with it.
---

I wanted this site to behave more like an index than a presentation. It should answer a few practical questions quickly: what I do, what I have worked on, and where to find the supporting details. Everything else has to earn its place.

That is why the home page stays in one view on desktop. The left side is a list that can be scanned without a long introduction or a sequence of calls to action. The right side is quiet until a project or the background note is selected. That gives the work enough room without turning the site into a gallery that asks to be admired before it can be understood.

Minimal does not mean removing every decision. It means making the decisions visible. Type needs to be readable. Links need a clear hover and focus state. Project names need to be easy to compare. Images need to support the project instead of carrying the entire page. A small amount of structure is more useful here than a decorative theme.

I also like the constraint this creates when adding something new. Before a section goes in, I have to decide whether it helps someone understand the work or whether it is only filling space. That is a useful question for product interfaces too. Most pages do not need another panel, another badge, or another explanation if the flow is already clear.

The site is intentionally plain, but not accidental. The warm background, restrained type system, and index layout are there to make the information feel considered without becoming precious. It is a place for a few projects and a few short entries, not a feed that needs constant activity.

Keeping the surface small also makes maintenance easier. There is less UI to keep consistent, fewer routes to explain, and less content that can go stale. The result is closer to the kind of software I prefer building: direct, calm, and useful when someone needs it.
